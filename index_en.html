Strategic value

What ITEA 4.0 contributes to the state of the art

The economics of AI's labour-market impact has been organised around two traditions that, until now, have run in parallel.

The first, task-centric (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2020; Acemoglu, Autor, Hazell and Restrepo, 2022; Autor et al., 2020), measures occupational exposure to automation and treats it as an exogenous productivity shock read in terms of displacement, wages and the labour share. The second, more recent —labor as capital— recognises that AI systems do not only substitute tasks: they absorb the worker's expertise and incorporate it as a firm asset, whether documenting empirically that generative models encode the knowledge of the most productive workers (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2025) or modelling the ownership of that expertise (Cullen, Li and Li, 2025). This second strand, however, lacked an occupation-scale measurement instrument.

ITEA 4.0 sits at the intersection of both traditions and fills that gap. Its contribution is threefold:

Conceptual

It reframes the phenomenon as algorithmic expropriation —the uncompensated transfer of the worker's intellectual capital to the firm's algorithmic assets— shifting the normative frame from job displacement to value appropriation.

Measurement

It operationalises that transfer as a task-level exposure index, built on O*NET and harmonised with ESCO via dense semantic embeddings: knowledge capture stops being a theoretical intuition and becomes a magnitude comparable across occupations, sectors and countries.

Policy

It links the measurement to an explicit compensation mechanism —tokenised residual rights (θ·V) versus a Pigouvian tax on algorithmic capital— bridging the empirical diagnosis and institutional design.

The hinge of the argument. ITEA inherits the task-centric measurement apparatus from the Acemoglu–Autor tradition, but redirects it from the dimension of substitution to that of capture and transfer —exactly what the Brynjolfsson–Li and Cullen–Li–Li strand identifies but does not instrument. It does not invent the phenomenon: it invents how to measure and compensate it.

From a national framework to a global family

v4.0 takes that apparatus beyond the United States: the European (ESCO) environment and the US–EU bridge show that the measurement is portable and comparable across systems. On that same architecture, national instances are underway for Japan, Korea and India (in development).

Key findings

What we found —and why it matters

Each finding is placed within the academic literature and translated into a concrete application for governments, firms and researchers. The charts are qualitative schematics or figures from public literature; they do not represent ITEA index values.

1 · AI acts on the activity, not the occupation

AI integration is selective: it penetrates specific tasks within jobs, not whole jobs. The correct unit of analysis is the activity.

Share of occupations with AI use by task-coverage threshold (Handa et al., 2025, public data).

Relevance (literature)

It refines the task-centric apparatus (Autor, Levy and Murnane, 2003; Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2020): occupational exposure is an aggregate that hides where AI actually lands.

Strategic application

It enables prioritising training, job redesign and policy by activity, at a resolution the occupation-level view cannot offer.

2 · Generative AI lands on knowledge, not on classic routine

Real AI use follows cognitive, codifiable work and is blind to classic routine automation. GenAI is not the old automation.

The "gradient turn": qualitative schematic (Frey and Osborne, 2017; Eloundou et al., 2024; Handa et al., 2025).

Relevance (literature)

It empirically distinguishes generative exposure from classic automatability indices (Frey and Osborne, 2017; Eloundou et al., 2024), validating the construct in its own unit.

Strategic application

It refocuses risk onto knowledge work —the most exposed and, at once, the most valuable to protect and compensate.

3 · There is a responsibility axis: augment vs. delegate

Use is ordered between augmentation (the professional retains judgement) and automation (the activity is delegated and codified). It is the automation side that is associated with job loss, per independent market evidence.

Augmentation / automation split of AI use (Anthropic Economic Index, public data).

Relevance (literature)

It operationalises what labor as capital names (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond, 2025; Cullen, Li and Li, 2025): not all AI is equal; delegation is what transfers value.

Strategic application

It identifies where to apply compensation mechanisms (θ·V) and where human–AI collaboration creates value without expropriating it.

4 · "Expropriated yet retained": displacement without dismissal

The worker's value can be transferred to the firm's algorithmic assets with no dismissal; the prevailing employment contract does not record that transfer.

Schematic of the appropriation gap: value captured versus value compensated (illustrative).

Relevance (literature)

It shifts the normative frame from displacement (unemployment, wages) to value appropriation, an angle the task-centric tradition does not capture.

Strategic application

It underpins contractual and income-policy redesign: measuring the capture is the first step to compensating it.

5 · The framework is portable and comparable across systems

The exposure ordering survives the O*NET↔ESCO bridge at every resolution (translation invariance), and ITEA's European structure even predicts real AI use in Europe.

Portability schematic: US (O*NET) and Europe (ESCO) exposure preserve the same ordering (illustrative).

Relevance (literature)

It provides behavioural external validation on the European axis —something absent in prior literature, anchored in US data.

Strategic application

It enables comparable multi-country deployment (EU, and underway Japan, Korea and India) on a single architecture.

Methodology

How expropriation is measured

ITEA decomposes each occupation into its tasks and activities (the unit where AI actually operates) and grades each one along three dimensions —generative exposure, knowledge codifiability and level of responsibility— which are aggregated into occupational indicators and a composite expropriation index. The framework carefully distinguishes exposure (how much of the work is reachable by AI) from expropriation (the transfer of intellectual capital), and validates the construct against independent external criteria (of potential exposure, realised employment and real AI use).

The harmonisation between the US taxonomy (O*NET) and the European one (ESCO) is done via dense semantic embeddings over each task statement, which lets the same construct be read in both systems and the US–EU bridge be built. The public methodological documentation details the apparatus, the indicators per environment and the validation.

ITEA 4.0 · Three environments

United States · Europe · Bridge

One construct, three readings on the common ISCO-08 axis. Each environment provides access to its documentation; the US environment also includes an interactive dashboard.

US · O*NET

ITEA 4.0 — United States

The framework's base (the v3.0 line, with targeted improvements): ten complementary indicators over O*NET 30.2 occupations and a composite expropriation index, with external convergent validation. The v4.0 novelty is validating the construct at the task level against real AI use observed in the market.

Europe · ESCO

ITEA 4.0 — Europe (ESCO)

The first European expropriation-exposure index, on ESCO v1.2.1 / ISCO-08. Qualification is anchored to the European Qualifications Framework (EQF) and the index is convergently validated against an independent European criterion. It adds a behavioural criterion by ESCO competence.

ITEA 4.0 — Bridge (US–EU)

The hybrid-portability environment between O*NET and ESCO on ISCO-08. It demonstrates translation invariance —the exposure ordering survives the bridge at every resolution— and enables cross-system comparisons: ITEA's European structure even predicts real AI use in Europe.
